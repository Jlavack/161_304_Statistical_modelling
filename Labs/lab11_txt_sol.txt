11E1. If an event has probability 0.35, what are the log-odds of this event?
$logit(p) = log(p/1-p)$

11E2. If an event has log-odds 3.2, what is the probability of this event?

This one is asking for the reverse operation compared to 11E1. Going from log-odds to probability uses the logistic function to accomplish this. Notice the \texttt{plogis()} function that is exactly the logistic function (and \texttt{qlogis()} would be the logit function):
$logit(p) = log(p/1-p)$
inverse logit $exp(p)/ (1+exp(p))$

$p = invlogit(3.2)$
$plogis(3.2) = 0.96$

11E3. Suppose that a coefficient in a logistic regression has value 1.7. What does this imply about the proportional change in odds of the outcome?
The proportional change in odds is calculated by exponentiating the coefficient:

$exp(1.7) = 5.47$

This means that each unit change in the predictor variable multiplies the odds of the event by approximately 5.5. Make sure you write the log-odds and the odds (hence with the exponential of the linear model $a+1.7 \times x$) to identify this change. Also remark that the change in probability depends on the value of the probability itself, so on the value of $a$, the intercept and of that of the predictor $x$ too, since the probability writes:
$$ p(x) = \frac{\exp(a+1.7 \times x)}{1+\exp(a+1.7 \times x)}. $$

13E2. Rewrite the following model as a multilevel model.

\begin{eqnarray*}
y_i & \sim & \mathrm{Binomial}(1, p_i) \\
\mathrm{logit}(p_i) & = & \alpha_{group[i]} + \Beta  x_{i}\\
\alpha_{group} & \sim & \mathrm{Normal}(0, 1.5) \\
\Beta & \sim & \mathrm{Normal}(0, 0.5) \\
\end{eqnarray*}


To turn the model into a multi-level model, we need to assign some hyper-priors. That is priors on parameters of likelihood distribution parameters. In this case, we express $\alpha_{group[i]}$ (a parameter) through a normal prior with another set of parameters: $\overline{\alpha}$ the mean, with a normal prior and $\sigma_{\alpha}$ with an Exponential prior. $\beta$ won't change here as it doesn't depend on the group. We could in theory...
\begin{eqnarray*}
y_i & \sim & \mathrm{Binomial}(1, p_i) \\
\mathrm{logit}(p_i) & = & \alpha_{group[i]} + \beta x_i \\
\alpha_{group} & \sim & \mathrm{Normal}(\overline{\alpha}, \sigma_{\alpha}) \\
\overline{\alpha} & \sim & \mathrm{Normal}(0,2) \\
\sigma_{\alpha} & \sim & \mathrm{Exponential}(1) \\
\beta & \sim & \mathrm{Normal}(0,1)
\end{eqnarray*}
The numbers we feed into our hyperpriors here are difficult to assess for sensibility since we don't have any data to test the performance of both models and their assumptions.

11M7. Use quap to construct a quadratic approximate posterior distribution for the chimpanzee model that includes a unique intercept for each actor, m11.4 (page 330). Compare the quadratic approximation to the posterior distribution produced instead from MCMC. Can you explain both the differences and the similarities between the approximate and the MCMC distributions? Relax the prior on the actor intercepts to Normal(0,10). Re-estimate the posterior using both ulam and quap. Do the differences increase or decrease? Why?
```{r}
data("chimpanzees")

chimpanzees$treatment <- 1 + chimpanzees$prosoc_left + 2*chimpanzees$condition

dat_list <- list(pulled_left = chimpanzees$pulled_left,
                 actor = chimpanzees$actor, 
                 treatment = chimpanzees$treatment)

q11.4 <- quap( alist(
  pulled_left ~ dbinom( 1 , p ),
  logit(p) <- a[actor] + b[treatment],
  a[actor] ~ dnorm(0, 1.5),
  b[treatment] ~ dnorm(0, 0.5)
), data=dat_list)

m11.4 <- ulam( alist(
  pulled_left ~ dbinom( 1 , p ),
  logit(p) <- a[actor] +b[treatment],
  a[actor] ~ dnorm(0, 1.5),
  b[treatment] ~ dnorm(0, 0.5)
), data=dat_list, chains = 2, log_lik =TRUE)
  
plot(precis(m11.4, depth = 2))
plot(precis(q11.4, depth = 2))
```
Now if we look quickly at a summary of the posterior, we see that there isn't much difference between the distribution of the parameters, either of the actors or for the treatments. The only parameter that shows a noticeable difference is Actor 2. Looking a little closer, we see that for Actor 2, MCMC results in a posterior shifted slightly to the right. This is because the MCMC posterior is slightly skewed, whereas the quadratic approximation	 posterior is forced to be Gaussian. Therefore, more density is given to the lower tail and less to the upper tail than in the MCMC posterior.


Now let's modify our prior distributions. By loosening the prior, we are letting the actor intercepts take even more extreme values. This should have the effect of letting the posterior become even more skewed.

I will use the \texttt{ulam()} estimates, to account for the small asymmetry.

First, the intercept log-odds, \texttt{a}, indicates the probability of a successful attempt for a pirate when all of the predictors are at zero. So that means a small immature pirate against a small victim has probability $\mathrm{logistic}(0.37) \approx 59\%$ of success.

Second, what about the slope estimates? These estimates just change the intercept for the log-odds. So for example, the probability that a large immature pirate succeeds against a small victim would be $\mathrm{logistic}(0.37+2.66) \approx 95.4\%$. That is to say, the large pirate has good chances to succeed, according to the model. 

Third, you can generate such predictions for all of the combinations, if you wish. And indeed, that's how the plotting of predictions works.

Before that, what about the proportional odds (odds ratio) interpretations? You can exponentiate each $\beta$ estimate to get the proportional change in odds that arising from changing the corresponding dummy variable from 0 to 1. Ignoring the confidence intervals for the moment (they will appear in the plots to come), you can quickly exponentiate the $\beta$ estimates with: \texttt{round(exp(coef(eagle\_ulam)[2:4]),3)}.
So this says that the odds $p/(1-p)$ of a successful attempt are multiplied by 14.2, when the pirate becomes large. That's a large effect. 

Similarly, the odds of success are multiplied by 0.06, when the victim becomes large. So if the pirate is large and the victim is large, the proportional change in odds is $14.2 \times 0.06 \approx 0.87 $ or about 13\% reduction in odds. 
Finally, the effect of being adult is to improve the odds, multiplying them by about 2.5. These are all relative risk measures. For the absolute measures, it will be nice to plot them.

       # interaction comes in via bP[size_]

    sigma ~ dexp(1),

```{r}
plot(coeftab(m_Tank, m_Pred, m_Size, m_Additive, m_Interaction),
  pars="sigma", labels=c("Tank", "Predation", "Size", "Additive", "Interaction")

```
Quite evidently, omitting \texttt{pred} (predation) from our models assigns a lot of variation to the tank variable. Conclusively, we can say that predation explains a lot of the variation across tanks and helps to explain it. Omitting predation from our models simply assigns this variation to the tank intercepts without explaining it. Another way to say it is to notice that \texttt{size\_} reduces the variation much less than does \texttt{pred}. The predictor \texttt{size\_}, in these models, doesn't help prediction very much, so accounting for it has minimal impact on the estimated variation across tanks. Predation does seem to matter, even if it doesn't explain much of the total variation.

Note that adding a predictor always decreased the posterior mean variation across tanks. Why? Because the predictors are, well, predicting variation. This leaves less variation for the varying intercepts to mop up. In theory, if we had in the form of predictor variables all of the relevant information that determined the survival outcomes, there would be zero variation across tanks.
